Flock cameras

License plate cameras and police drones

In October 2025 the City Council approved a contract with Flock Group Inc. for license plate reader cameras and a drone program for the San Angelo Police Department. This page sets out what was approved, how the system works, the constitutional questions, what supporters and critics say, and where this site stands, including a proposed Technology Bill of Rights for the City and County.

Updated Oct 29, 2026Contract approved Oct 2025

Our view. privacy is a human right. We support plate cameras that the City owns outright, kept on a local network cut off from the internet, and searched only for an APB or a warrant. We also propose a Technology Bill of Rights for the City and County.

How the system works

Each camera photographs passing vehicles and reads the plate. Flock's software also records details such as make, model, and color, which police can use as search terms. Plates are checked against hotlists, such as stolen vehicles and Amber Alerts, and a match sends an alert to officers.118

Records are kept for a number of days each agency sets; Flock recommends 7. An agency can let other agencies search its records through Flock's statewide and national lookup networks. Flock says sharing is off by default, that each agency controls whom it shares with, and that every search is logged with the agency and a stated reason.11819

A drone first responder program launches a docked drone toward a 911 call, so police can see the scene before officers arrive.5

The constitutional questions

General information about court rulings, not legal advice.

The Fourth Amendment protects "the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures," and says no warrant shall issue but upon probable cause, particularly describing what is to be searched. The Texas Constitution makes the same promise in Article I, Section 9.1011

The question for plate cameras is whether photographing every passing car, and keeping those records to search later, is itself a search. If it is, police generally need a warrant to look. Courts have not settled it for camera networks. Since 2012, though, the Supreme Court has extended Fourth Amendment protection to records of where a person goes.121320

How the courts have ruled

  1. 1983United States v. Knotts. Following a car on public roads with a radio beeper was not a search: "A person travelling in an automobile on public thoroughfares has no reasonable expectation of privacy in his movements from one place to another." The Court left open whether "dragnet type law enforcement practices" would call for different rules. Courts upholding plate cameras still lean on this case.2122
  2. 2012United States v. Jones. Attaching a GPS tracker to a car and following it for 28 days was a search. Justice Alito, writing for four justices, said "longer term GPS monitoring in investigations of most offenses impinges on expectations of privacy."20
  3. 2018Carpenter v. United States. Getting a person's historical cell phone location records was a search, and police needed a warrant. The Court said people have "a reasonable expectation of privacy in the whole of their physical movements," and that seven days of records was enough to count.12
  4. 2020Commonwealth v. McCarthy (Massachusetts high court). Four plate cameras at the ends of two bridges were not a search. But "with enough cameras in enough locations," the court said, the location history from a plate reader system "would constitute a search."14
  5. Jan 2026Schmidt v. City of Norfolk (federal court, Virginia). A judge upheld Norfolk's Flock network, finding it did not invade a reasonable expectation of privacy because the cameras do not capture the whole of a person's movements. The residents appealed; the case was pending at the Fourth Circuit as of June 2026.62223
  6. Jun 2026Chatrie v. United States. The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that getting a person's phone location records from Google was a search, even for a short window: "It does not matter if the time period scrutinized was only two hours." The case is about phone data, not cameras, but it rejects the idea that a small slice of a large location database is not a search.13

The case for and against

The arguments as each side makes them in the national debate over Flock. Our own view follows in the next section.

What supporters say

  • Police credit plate readers with tracking down stolen cars and helping solve high-profile crimes.1
  • SAPD publishes a plate reader transparency portal and a drone flight dashboard for the public.2
  • State grants cover most of the cost.3
  • A drone can reach a scene before officers do, which can tell them what they are driving into.45

What critics raise

  • The cameras record every passing car, not only suspects, and the records can be searched without a warrant. That is the claim in a federal lawsuit against Norfolk, Virginia.6
  • Several cities learned their data was shared more widely than they knew, and audits found local searches that appeared to be for immigration enforcement.1
  • In Texas, sheriff's deputies searched the network for the car of a woman who had an abortion, according to a 404 Media report.17
  • At least 30 local governments turned off Flock cameras or canceled contracts between the start of 2025 and February 2026.1

Flock's response

Flock says it ended its federal pilot programs, that immigration authorities have no direct access to its platform, and that in June 2025 it added filters that block searches for immigration or reproductive health terms where state law bars them. It later required officers to pick an offense type before searching.18

Where we stand

This section is our opinion, labeled as such. The rest of this page sticks to the record.

Opinion

Privacy is a human right. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights says "no one shall be subjected to arbitrary interference with his privacy," and the Fourth Amendment and the Texas Constitution protect it here. The best systems catch car thieves and find missing children without keeping a searchable record of where everyone else drives.91011

Protecting the Fourth Amendment matters most when the technology is new. The courts are still deciding where plate cameras fall, and the trend favors privacy. San Angelo can lead on this instead of waiting for a ruling.121314

We support localizing plate cameras. Keep the cameras, but bring the system home: owned by the City, run on a closed local network, and opened only when there is cause.

Working with SAPD

Since 2024, Spencer Matthews, who writes this site, has met multiple times with the San Angelo Police Department about these cameras: with the Police Chief's office, the assistant police chief, and the department's technology team.

They care about privacy as much as we do. Every one of those conversations has been about the same goal: a system that keeps residents safe and protects their rights.

We think highly of them. They are working to put ideas like these into practice and to lead San Angelo toward a human-centered future, while keeping officers safe and the city ready for any emergency it may face.

The author's own account of the meetings. Spencer builds AI systems and is helping SAPD decide how to choose a local company to build this system. Read the disclosure

How a local system would work

  1. 1

    Air-gapped

    Cameras report to a local server on a closed network, cut off from the internet and from outside data-sharing networks.

  2. 2

    APB or warrant only

    The system checks plates against active APBs and warrants. Only an APB or a warrant opens the live feeds or the stored photos to a search.

  3. 3

    Kept 30 days

    Photos are deleted after 30 days. Outside an APB or a warrant, the record cannot be pulled.

  4. 4

    Owned by the City

    SAPD owns the hardware, the software, and the data, with no subscription fees and safeguards against abuse built in.

A local agency has already demonstrated a working prototype. Building it locally means the police department owns the system outright, stops paying recurring fees, and keeps control of our data. Today the City's grant budget includes Flock monitoring fees for its plate cameras.3

We believe San Angelo would be one of the first cities in the world to run plate cameras this way. That is a story worth telling about our city.

Technology should protect and serve our officers. To do that, the City has to own these assets. West Texas was built by self-sufficient people who kept control of what they depended on, and our public safety tools should be no different.

The switch would take about a year: time for the current contracts to run out and for the new system to be built and tested. The current Flock agreement was approved Oct 7, 2025.15

A Technology Bill of Rights

Our proposal for the City of San Angelo and Tom Green County.

Proposal

We propose that the City Council and the Commissioners Court each adopt a Technology Bill of Rights for residents. Technology changes faster than the law, so these rules set the limits before the next tool is bought, not after.

People stay in charge

Machines can help. People decide, and people speak for the government.

  1. 1
    No autonomous robots. No autonomous robots of any type. Every robot or drone the City or County uses is operated by a person or assisted by one in real time.
  2. 2
    A person makes the call. No AI makes the final decision on a resident's fine, permit, benefit, or arrest. A named person decides, and residents can appeal to a person.
  3. 3
    Real people only. No City or County communication uses synthetic or AI-generated people or voices in video, photos, or audio. Only real people speak for the City or County in an official capacity.
  4. 4
    AI is labeled. Any chatbot, phone line, or form run by AI says so up front. Records and police reports drafted with AI are labeled and signed by the person responsible.
  5. 5
    No predictive policing. No software that scores or flags individuals as likely to commit a crime.

Your data stays home

Data collected on residents belongs to the public and stays under local control.

  1. 6
    Stored in San Angelo. Data the City and County collect on residents is stored within the city limits.
  2. 7
    Never sold or shared. Resident data is never sold, and never shared with vendors or other agencies without a warrant or court order, except where state or federal law requires it. No vendor may use it to train AI.
  3. 8
    Government owns its data. Every technology contract gives the City or County full ownership of its data, the right to export it, and the right to have the vendor delete it. No lock-in.
  4. 9
    Collect less. Collect only what a service needs, publish how long each kind of record is kept, and delete it on schedule.
  5. 10
    Breach notice. Residents are told within days when their data is exposed, and told what was exposed.

Surveillance answers to the public

No new way of watching residents without a public vote and public rules.

  1. 11
    No face recognition. No facial, voice, or gait recognition on City or County cameras or recordings without a warrant.
  2. 12
    Vote before buying. Any new surveillance tool needs a public hearing, a published use policy, and a vote of the City Council or Commissioners Court before it is bought.
  3. 13
    Public inventory. A public list of every surveillance tool: what it collects, what it costs, and how long its data is kept, with an annual audit report.

Everyone can reach their government

No one is shut out for not owning the latest device.

  1. 14
    Right to a person. Every City and County service can be reached by a real person, by phone or at a counter.
  2. 15
    Cash and paper. Taxes, bills, and fines can be paid in cash or by mail, and no service requires a smartphone or a digital ID.

School district. We ask San Angelo ISD to adopt the same rules for student data and school cameras.

A proposal, not law. Each right would need a vote of the City Council or the Commissioners Court, and state law may limit how some of them can be written.
